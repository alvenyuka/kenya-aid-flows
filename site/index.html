How much of the aid scheduled for Kenya arrives in its year?

For 2,901 activity-years with a full-year annual budget, 73% of the $4.63 billion scheduled was disbursed in the year it was scheduled for, 2010 to 2024. A data pipeline built step by step from open aid records: download, clean, SQL models, checks, a simple rule and a model.

Results

What arrived, and what did not

Share of scheduled money that arrived in its own year, on full-year annual budgets.

The headline

73% of the scheduled money arrived in its own year: $3.40 billion of $4.63 billion. Any amount above plan is not counted, so one project that over-spent cannot hide another that fell short. A variant that also penalises overshoot gives 60%.

Total disbursements were $4.45 billion, a ratio of 0.96 to plan. The middle activity-year sent 0.95 of its plan, 58.8% fell short (under 99%), 34.6% went over (above 101%) and 6.7% were within 1% of plan.

What the measure is

A variant inspired by the Paris Declaration and GPEDC indicator 5a (annual predictability: aid disbursed within the year it was scheduled). It uses calendar years, not Kenya's July to June fiscal year, works at activity level, includes NGO publishers, and does not penalise overshoot in the main figure.

An activity-year is one project in one calendar year. It is compared only if the donor's original annual budget lines cover the whole year, the plan is above zero and at least one disbursement is reported.

UNHCR is set aside, and it matters. The UN refugee agency publishes needs-based budgets and reports most of its spending as expenditure, not disbursement. Its 8 activity-years carry $1.35 billion of planned money, and only 20% of it arrived as disbursement. With UNHCR left in, 61% arrives and the ratio is 0.79. The rule that sets it aside was written after looking at UNHCR's numbers.

Budget lines with no stated type count as original in the main figure, because the IATI standard says “If the @type attribute is omitted, then BudgetType code 1 (Original) is assumed” (IATI standard, budget element). The stated-type version drops them.

The zero-counted version is a lower bound: a zero disbursement can mean the publisher reports spending as expenditure (code E). Multi-year budgets are not a plan for any one year, so their ratio says little.

Rule and model

Can a model beat a simple rule?

The model ranks the activity-years a little better than the rule (a higher PR-AUC), but at the same number of flags it is not more precise and does not catch more, so it does not beat the rule on the flags themselves.

Both look only at earlier years. The model was trained on 779 activity-years before 2020, with the activity's earlier ratio, its number of earlier years, plan size, the publisher's earlier record and the sector. Nothing was tuned on the test years. The publisher's record keeps updating through the test years, using strictly earlier years only, like the activity's own earlier ratio: a 2022 row uses the outcomes of 2020 and 2021. Precision is the share of flags that were right, recall is the share of true cases caught, and PR-AUC summarises how well the scores rank true cases above others. Accuracy is not shown, because flagging nothing would score well on it.

The rule of thumb on its own flags 67% correctly against a 31% base rate. A second version that counts a missing disbursement as zero reaches 77% precision, but 132 of its 263 correct flags are years with no disbursement row, so it partly detects activities that stop reporting.

Method

How it is built, in plain words

One folder per stage, small scripts, plain SQL files.

1. Download. 26,438 activities, 469,065 transactions and 113,136 budget lines for Kenya from the d-portal query API, each table checked against the API's own row count.

2. Clean. One small rule per function, each logging the rows and dollars it removes: incoming-fund rows that would count money twice, non-aid flow types (private investment such as IFC), impossible dates, exact duplicates, negative corrections and budget rows that are planned disbursements.

3. Load and model in SQL. The cleaned tables go into DuckDB. Plain numbered SQL files cut each budget line into calendar-year pieces by days, test whether annual lines cover the whole year, and build the activity-year table of planned, disbursed and committed money.

4. Set aside three publishers whose budgets cannot be compared with their spending. The Gates Foundation's budgets repeat its disbursements (1,090 activity-years within 1% of plan). CGIAR's budgets cover only a slice of its spending (median ratio 4.47). UNHCR's are needs-based. The thresholds (100 or more activity-years for the first two; for UNHCR at least 3 years, under 30% disbursed in every year, mostly untyped budget lines, and more expenditure than disbursement) were chosen after looking at the data.

5. Check. 10 SQL queries that must return no rows, including that cutting budgets into years keeps every dollar and that every compared year is covered day by day. The script fails if any check returns a row.

6. Rule and model. A rule of thumb first, then a logistic regression judged against it on the same rows.

Limits

What this result does not show

Read these before quoting a number.

  • Only 2,901 of 46,232 activity-years can be compared, and publishers that give full-year budgets are not a random sample of aid to Kenya.
  • The comparison is against the original budget. Budgets are often revised, so a project can look short when its plan changed.
  • Removing incoming funds does not remove every double count: if a donor and its implementer both report one disbursement, both stay.
  • Negative disbursements and commitments are dropped, not netted, which raises totals slightly.
  • A zero disbursement can be a reporting habit, because some publishers report spending only as expenditure.
  • The publisher thresholds were chosen after seeing the data, and other unusual publishers may remain.
  • The yearly totals have not been compared with published Kenya aid figures. Sector names come from a list typed from the OECD DAC codes.
  • The rule and model are scored on 706 activity-years, so small differences between them are within noise.
  • Years end in 2024: disbursements dated 2025 are 25% below 2024 while 2025 budgets are the largest of any year, which looks like late reporting.
